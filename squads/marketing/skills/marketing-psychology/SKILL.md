# Marketing psychology

Why a piece works is a mechanism, not a vibe. Pick one to three principles that fit the audience's awareness stage and the piece's single action, write them into the copy, then run the ethics check. A principle that cannot survive the ethics check is not used.

## When to Use

- Writing or reviewing copy, hooks, offers, pricing or a call to action.
- A piece feels flat and the fix is not wording but framing.
- Choosing between two directions (anchor high vs. low, scarcity vs. no scarcity).
- Before shipping anything with an offer, a price or a proof claim.

## Procedure

1. **Locate the audience.** Awareness (unaware → problem-aware → solution-aware → product-aware → most aware) and market sophistication. Write to the next step of that journey, not to the end of it.
2. **Name the mechanism.** From the list below, choose one to three that fit, and say which in one line. More than three compete with each other.
3. **Write it in.** Framing goes into the headline; the mechanism goes into the proof; the closing goes into one action. One message, one call to action.
4. **Check the facts.** Numbers, results and testimonials need a verified `brand.md` proof point with permission; everything else is cut.
5. **Ethics check, then ship.** Any of the following means rewrite or drop: false scarcity or urgency, fabricated social proof, testimonials without permission, misleading defaults or hidden conditions, fake countdowns, invented figures, pressure aimed at vulnerable groups, imitated people or brands.

## Core principles

| Principle | Use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Awareness level | The message must meet the audience where it is |
| Market sophistication | Claim-weary markets need a new mechanism, not a bigger claim |
| Hook → story → offer | Any long-form piece: grab, hold, ask |
| Value equation | The offer feels expensive: raise the outcome, lower the effort or risk |
| Benefit over feature | Always; features only as proof of the benefit |
| Specificity | A number or a detail beats an adjective |
| One action | Every piece has exactly one call to action |
| Reduced friction | Attention is cheap, action is costly: fewer steps, clearer next move |
| Social proof | Real customers, real volume, permission recorded |
| Loss aversion | Showing what the audience loses by waiting — only when the loss is real |
| Anchoring | Prices, tiers and comparison points; the first number frames the rest |
| Real scarcity | Only what the brand can prove: stock, dates, seats |
| Goal gradient | Progress feels close: "two of three steps done" |
| Peak-end | The last exchange before the ask is what they remember |
| Mere exposure | A repeated signal, on a schedule, stays recognizable |
| Pratfall | An honest, small admission raises trust in a boast-heavy feed |
| Distinctiveness | Assets the audience recognizes without the logo |
| Emotion and reason | Emotion to move, reason to justify the move afterwards |
| Curse of knowledge | Say it as the audience first hears it, not as the brand knows it |

## Pitfalls

- Stacking ten principles into one piece until nothing lands.
- Urgency the brand cannot back with a real date or stock number.
- Social proof borrowed from a competitor or a different audience.
- Treating the ethics check as a final polish; it is a gate.
- Copying a tactic that works for another awareness level.

## Verification

- The piece names its one to three mechanisms and its single call to action.
- Every claim traces to a verified proof point with permission.
- The ethics check ran with zero flagged items; otherwise the piece was rewritten or dropped.
- The tone matches `brand.md` voice rules, not the principle's novelty.
