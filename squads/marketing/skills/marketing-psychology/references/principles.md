## 1. Audience stage

- **Awareness levels** (Schwartz): unaware → problem-aware → solution-aware → product-aware → most aware. A piece that sells a solution to a problem-unaware audience fails; the next step is the only target.
- **Market sophistication**: in a claim-weary market, lead with the mechanism, not with a bigger claim. In a young market, name the problem first.
- **Curse of knowledge**: the brand knows too much. Write the first sentence for someone who has never heard the offer.

## 2. Message mechanisms

- **Hook → story → offer**: grab with the unresolved thing, hold with the story, ask once.
- **Value equation** (Hormozi): perceived value = outcome x likelihood ÷ time x effort. Cheap move: raise likelihood (proof, guarantee) instead of discounting.
- **Benefit over feature**: the feature is the proof of the benefit, not the message.
- **Specificity**: "15 minutes" beats "fast"; a named street beats "central".
- **One action**: people do one thing; the second call to action taxes the first.
- **Friction**: every extra step costs conversions; remove fields, taps and thinking.
- **Social proof** (Cialdini): reviews, counts, badges — real ones, with permission. Volume is the message.
- **Loss aversion**: what is lost is felt twice as hard as what is gained; use it with a real loss, never a manufactured one.
- **Anchoring**: the first number sets the scale; show the reference before the price.
- **Real scarcity**: only what the brand can prove; fake timers are a veto item.
- **Goal gradient**: people finish what they have started; show progress, proof, next step.
- **Peak-end**: the end of the experience colors the memory; the last message before the ask matters more than the first.
- **Mere exposure**: repeat one clear signal on a schedule; recognition precedes preference.
- **Pratfall**: a small, honest admission ("we're not the cheapest") buys trust in a boast-heavy feed.
- **Distinctive assets**: color, shape, face, phrase recognized without the logo.
- **Emotion then reason**: emotion moves the action; the reason arrives afterwards to justify it. Both are needed, in that order.
- **Commitment and consistency**: a small yes before the ask (a reply, a sample, a short form).
- **Framing**: "9 of 10 renew" and "1 in 10 leaves" are the same fact; pick the true, honest frame.
- **Decoy and contrast**: comparing against a clearly worse option changes the middle choice — only with honest options, all usable.
- **Reciprocity**: give something usable before asking; it works once, not as a loop.

## 3. Offer and pricing treatments

- **Charm pricing vs. round prices**: charm ("$19.900") reads as a deal; round ("$20.000") reads as premium. Match the brand's position.
- **Rule of 100**: under 100, a percentage off feels bigger; over 100, an absolute amount does.
- **Price relativity**: one price means nothing alone; good-better-best makes each price legible.
- **Mental accounting**: reframe a cost per use or per month when the number is honest.
- **Bundles**: group what is bought together anyway; do not invent value with filler items.
- **Trial and guarantee**: move risk from the buyer to the brand; a guarantee must be one the brand will honor.

## 4. Delivery and design

- **Hick's law**: more options slow the choice; one visible action per screen or slide.
- **Salience**: the eye goes to contrast and motion before text; make the offer the most salient thing.
- **Reading order**: background and scene, one hero, then the action — see the assets skill for the visual rule.
- **Repetition with variation**: the same signal in new formats, not the same piece republished.

## 5. Ethics: the veto list

The following are never acceptable, whatever the principle: false scarcity or urgency, fabricated reviews, testimonials or counts, testimonials without permission, hidden conditions, misleading defaults, fake countdowns or stock counters, invented figures or sources, pressure aimed at vulnerable groups, imitated people or brands. If the honest version of a piece loses too much, the problem is the offer, not the ethics.
